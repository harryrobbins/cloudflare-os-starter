Architecture review · Records datastore service

What it would cost to pull Records out of cloudflare-os

Records is already built to be portable in its core. The coupling that remains sits in three places: how people get an identity, how data is managed, and how gadget writes are approved. This report maps those seams, prices four levels of separation, and lists what the core platform gives up at each one.

Verdict

Separate the code now and keep one deployment. Records should stay a Worker inside the cloudflare-os deploy (level 0) while its package boundaries are hardened to the point where lifting it out (level 1) is a configuration change. Do not start a standalone product (level 2) or a hosted service (level 3) until a second real consumer exists. Those levels are mostly new product work that cloudflare-os does not need, and they add running cost and a security surface that one team would carry alone.

  • Low regret, about 4 to 6 engineer-weeks: move the HTTP adapters into their own package, add an HTTP management API behind the existing permission matrix, and give the Node runtime an outbox publisher and scheduler.
  • The hardest seam is approvals. A gadget write is gated by a viewer assertion consumed from the Workshop's approval queue in-process. Out of process, that becomes a cross-service contract with no equivalent outside cfos.
  • The largest new cost is identity. Outside cfos there is no login, no sign-up and no self-service identity mapping. Organisations are created by an operator CLI.

Where the seams are today

About 14,000 lines of Records code have no Cloudflare imports. The Cloudflare Worker package, gatekeeper-records, is about 8,900 lines. Roughly a third of that is the Data management page, a third is the gadget vendor and connect flow, and the rest is HTTP adapters and Cloudflare runtime glue. Four levels of separation

Each level includes the ones before it. Effort is in engineer-weeks for one engineer who knows the codebase, and is an estimate. Running costs describe what drives the bill rather than quoting prices.

L0Today

One Worker inside the cfos deploy

Records is cfos-records, deployed by pnpm release with everything else, reached through the router under /gatekeeper/records/*. The portable packages exist and a Node entry point passes the same contract suite.

Build
Nothing new. Outstanding work is operational: migrations 0003 to 0009, a paid Neon plan (the Free plan keeps 6 hours of history), the R2 backup bucket and a restore rehearsal.
Running cost
Neon plus a share of the Workers plan. Hyperdrive, Queues, the rate limiter and Durable Objects ride on the existing account.
cfos gives up
Nothing. Records shares Access, the router, deploy order and observability.
L1Own service

Separate package tree, Worker and release train

Records moves to its own workspace or repository and deploys on its own schedule. The cfos gatekeeper talks to it over HTTP with delegated tokens only, exactly as the canonical plan intends.

Build
  • Extract @records/http from gatekeeper-records: 1 week
  • Own CI, release and deploy script, own Access application and route: 1 to 2 weeks
  • Replace the in-process DatastoreHandle with an HTTP client, including a design for the sync push approval gate across services: 2 to 3 weeks
  • Contract versioning policy and cross-version tests between gadgets, gatekeeper and service: 1 week
Total: about 5 to 7 weeks.
Running cost
Small. Another Worker, possibly on the same account. A second Access application. Extra requests per gadget call, because gatekeeper to service becomes a network hop.
cfos gives up
In-process calls, one release, and one deploy order. Gains a clean contract.
L2Product

A standalone Records product that anyone can run

Records works fully without cfos: people sign in, organisations create themselves, admins manage datastores in a console, and webhooks fire on any host.

Build
  • HTTP management API for the 30 DataManagement methods, in OpenAPI and the SDKs: 2 to 3 weeks
  • Admin console, porting the Data page off its Workshop bridge: 4 to 6 weeks
  • People identity: OIDC sign-in, sessions, invitation e-mail, self-service identity mapping, organisation sign-up: 4 to 6 weeks
  • Node outbox publisher, webhook delivery and a scheduler for partitions and pruning: 2 weeks
  • An approvals story: either none (every write applies or fails) or a native approval queue with its own UI: 0 or 3 to 5 weeks
  • Self-hosting guide, Docker image, upgrade policy for migrations: 2 weeks
Total: about 14 to 24 weeks on top of L1.
Running cost
An e-mail sender, an identity provider or Access for SaaS seats, a separate Neon project per environment, R2 for backups, and uptime monitoring for a public endpoint.
cfos gives up
Its management page becomes one of two front ends, and identity exists in two directories that must be kept in step.
L3Hosted SaaS

Multi-tenant service with cfos as one integration

Records is sold and operated as a service. cfos is a customer of it, on the same terms as any other integration.

Build
  • Billing, usage metering and plan limits: 4 to 8 weeks
  • Tenant isolation beyond RLS on org_id, such as a database or branch per large tenant: 4 to 6 weeks
  • Regions and data residency (per-region deploys and Neon projects): 4 weeks or more
  • Security programme: penetration test, access reviews, incident process, audit readiness: months of calendar time plus external fees
  • Status page, support tooling, abuse controls and rate tiers: 3 to 4 weeks
Total: 6 to 12 months before launch, then a standing on-call rota.
Running cost
Grows with tenants and regions. The largest items are people (on-call, support), audits and per-region databases, not compute.
cfos gives up
Control of the roadmap and of its own data plane. Its organisation data lives in a service run on someone else's priorities, even if that someone is the same team.

What the core platform gives up

These costs fall on cloudflare-os and its users, not on Records. The first level at which each one appears is marked.

Approvals become an integration contract

L1 · hardest

Today a gadget write is checked by consuming a one-use viewer assertion from the Workshop's approval queue, in the same call stack as the write. Out of process, Records has to trust a token saying "approved" or call back into the Workshop. Either way the pending outcome, its action ID and the later syncApprovals check become a versioned protocol between two services.

Outside cfos there is no approval queue at all, so a standalone Records either drops approvals or builds its own.

Access stops being the only front door

L2

Every Records route is currently behind the router and Cloudflare Access. A standalone service has a public endpoint that relies on its own credential checks alone, as the Node server's access: "none" option allows. The attack surface grows to include sign-in, invitation links and the management API, and the pen-test and monitoring work grows with it.

Two identity directories

L2

Records keeps its own principals, mapped from issuers through records.identity_mappings. cfos keeps Workshop accounts. Separate sign-in means a person can exist in one and not the other, change e-mail in one, or leave the organisation in one first. Offboarding needs to reach both, or SCIM, which is more work again.

Extra network hop on every gadget call

L1

Gatekeeper to service becomes an HTTP request with token verification on each side. The service already sits a network hop from Neon in eu-west-2; placement and Hyperdrive latency are still unmeasured (canonical plan §10, phase 0). The optimistic sync client hides most of this for the board, but reads in gadget server code will feel it.

Version skew between gadgets and the service

L1

With one release, contracts, SDK, gatekeeper and blueprints always match. With two release trains, a published blueprint may call a service one version ahead or behind. That needs a compatibility window, deprecation notices and tests that run old clients against new servers.

Deploy coordination

L1

Today migrations run before pnpm release, and deployOrder() deploys Records before the router that binds it. Across two pipelines, the order becomes a runbook step: Records migrations, then Records, then cfos. A mistake leaves cfos pointing at a service without the routes it needs, and the release gate in CLAUDE.md no longer covers it.

Management UX splits

L2

The Data page lives inside the Workshop, next to the gadgets that use the data. A standalone console duplicates it. Keeping both means shipping each management feature twice; dropping the Data page sends cfos users to another site with another sign-in.

Failure isolation cuts both ways

L1

A Records outage no longer shares a deploy with the rest of cfos, and a bad cfos deploy cannot break Records. But gadgets that depend on Records now fail on a dependency cfos cannot roll back itself, and a partial outage needs a clear error in every gadget that uses it.

Observability and on-call across two systems

L1

One user action crosses the Workshop, the gatekeeper, the service and Postgres. Following it needs a shared trace or request ID through the token exchange. With two services, someone has to own each pager.

Keeping the gadget path fast

L1

Board and report rely on pokes from the DatastorePokeHub Durable Object, which the gatekeeper reaches today through a direct binding. In a separate service, gadget poke hooks need a subscription across the boundary, or the gatekeeper polls. Either adds latency or moving parts.

Residency and compliance

L3

Organisation data already leaves Cloudflare for Neon on AWS London. A hosted product must state where each tenant's data lives, offer regions, and answer customer security questionnaires. cfos inherits those promises as a tenant rather than setting its own.

A larger test matrix

L1

The contract suite already runs on workerd and Node against one database. Separation adds cross-version tests, management API tests, identity-provider tests and, at L2, a self-hosted upgrade path. pnpm check gets slower or splits in two.

Focus

every level

L2 and L3 are mostly generic SaaS plumbing: sign-up, billing, e-mail, consoles, audits. None of it improves cfos. Every week spent there is a week not spent on the signed-in production checks, the pending migrations, or the next module.

Recommendation and next steps

Hold at L0 for deployment. Do the following, which make L1 cheap later and improve Records now, whatever happens.

  1. Extract @records/http (native API, OpenAPI, Jira handler, Access check) from gatekeeper-records, so records-node no longer depends on the cfos package. About 1 week.
  2. Add an HTTP management API for the DataManagement methods, behind the same permission matrix, and have the Data page call it through the existing capability. Useful for automation and scripts now. About 2 to 3 weeks.
  3. Give Node an outbox publisher and scheduler for webhooks, partition creation and replay-table pruning, so the portable runtime is complete. About 1 to 2 weeks.
  4. Write down the approval contract between gatekeeper and service before anything is split: what a pending outcome means, who holds the action ID, how approval reaches the service. Design only, a few days.
  5. Measure the hop. Finish the phase 0 latency measurements through deployed Hyperdrive with and without placement, so L1's latency cost is a number rather than a guess.
  6. Set a trigger for L1: a second consumer that is not cfos, or a need to deploy Records on a different schedule. Until then, one deployment is cheaper and safer.

Before any of this, the operational backlog still comes first: migrations 0003 to 0009 on Neon, a paid Neon plan, the R2 backup bucket, a restore rehearsal and the signed-in checks.
