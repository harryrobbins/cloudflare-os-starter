A: incentive. Judges also back the name they expect to win; an early correct pick earns more.

B: incentive + Jev. As A, with TypeSafe's Jev decision model (via OpenRouter) as the third stage of every round.

C: panel only. No backing, no points and no Jev. The control.

Own names are never revealed. A judge's scores for its own names are dropped from the tally and kept only to measure self-preference. Every call went through the LiteLLM proxy on a capped key, and each call's tokens and cost were logged.

    League v2

    A better process, tested cheaply

    What we changed after the reviews and a second literature review (PeerRank, PRD, PiCO, Language Model Council, ChatEval, tournament design), and what four small experiments showed. The experiments cost $1.63 in total.

    The v2 design

    1. Screen ownability, don't score it. Check the bare word and the suffixed form mechanically; blocked names or names with no usable domain are out before judging. The dossier the judges see holds facts only (registry, domains, meaning, existing uses), with no evaluative wording.
    2. Diversify judges by lens, not temperature. Give each judge a functional persona (CFO, CISO, brand strategist, trade-mark attorney, journalist, linguist, investor, procurement, operations, COO), rotated across models so persona and model effects can be separated. Leave temperature at the provider default: the test below and the literature find it adds noise, not independence.
    3. Exclude own and same-family names from each judge's ballot. In v1, only a judge's own names were dropped.
    4. Weight judges, and publish the unweighted result beside it. PeerRank gives more voting power to judges whose own proposals the others rate highly (damping 0.85). The alternative is reliability weighting: agreement with the other judges, damping 0.4, capped at 2/N. The next step is gold anchor names with a known order, which the literature recommends over both.
    5. Score absolutely to filter, then rank pairwise. One absolute round cuts the field to six to eight. Then every pair is judged in both orders and fitted with Bradley–Terry, with Copeland as a check. A verdict that flips when the order is swapped counts as a tie.
    6. Pay predictions with a proper scoring rule. Before the finals, each judge gives a probability for each finalist, scored with log and Brier rules, plus a "surprisingly popular" flag. This replaces the flat reward for backing the leader.
    7. Report reliability, not just a winner: effective judges, flip rate, position bias, bootstrap P(top-1), and how much weighting moved the ranking.

    Test 1: personas and temperature

    One blind absolute round over all 31 names, with the facts-only dossier (bare forms included), run twice per configuration. Stability is the Kendall τ between the two repeats; "vs base" is τ against the base ranking.

    Test 2: PeerRank and reliability weights

    Average judge weight across the eight test rounds (equal weight would be 0.100). PeerRank favours judges whose own names scored well; reliability weighting favours judges who agree with the rest.
