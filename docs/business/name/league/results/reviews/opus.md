## 1. The chosen name

**Countermand Labs** fits the brief well. It names the one thing competitors can't easily claim: a person's authority to stop an agent. It's a real English word, easy to spell, sober, and every checked domain is free. The risks:
- **It's negative and military.** It names a brake, not what the firm builds. The company sells working applications, and "Countermand" suggests a firm that overrules its clients' orders. Judges raised the military tone in every final.
- **"Labs" works against the gravitas.** It sounds experimental to a CFO. It's also why the name came back clear: the bare word "Countermand" was never checked at Companies House, for domains, or at UKIPO.
- **It isn't wry.** The brief asked for "a little wry", and no criterion scored that.

I'd shortlist it but not pick it yet. First I'd run a UKIPO check and test it on 5–10 real CFOs and CISOs for recall after one hearing. The league can't measure memorability, however many times it runs.

## 2. Other names you like

- **Penstock.** A penstock is a sluice gate that meters a river, which is controlled flow, and it sounds civilian and engineered. The league undervalued it. "Pentera" is a weak sound-alike, and Penstock Group is US healthcare software, a different field.
- **Chary.** It's the only name that's actually wry, and it's short and ownable. Worries that people would read it as "charity" cost it more than they should have.
- **Imprimatur.** It means official approval, which is exactly "waits for human sign-off", and it has real weight. The Catholic-censorship association is a real cost, but a small one for this audience.
- **Countersign** (not in the league, unchecked). It means a second signature before an action goes ahead, which is the human-approval step put positively. Worth checking against Countermand.

## 3. How effective the process was

**What worked:**
- It cheaply cut 31 names to a sensible top six for $7.
- The dossier removed names that are unusable (blocked or crowded).
- Critiques were specific.

**What the measures actually show:**
- **Judge correlation.** It was 0.88, which works out to about 1.1 effective judges, or 1.3 counting only names that were legally clear. Ten models behaved like one judge, and mostly they were agreeing with the dossier. Critiques often repeat its wording, e.g. "military tone" and "Pentera".
- **Herding.** About 0 in all variants. Judges rescored by around 0.46 points, but not towards the shared mean. That's reassuring, but narrow: it doesn't measure influence from critiques or advocates.
- **Jev.** Correlation with the panel was 0.97, so giving Jev the same weight as the whole panel added no independent information. Variant B went 5–0 to Countermand, which fits Jev amplifying the consensus.
- **Backing.** Models picked the previous round's leader 3 or 4 times out of 4. That's following momentum, not prediction. With a flat 16/8/4/2/1 reward, the best strategy is to copy the leader.
- **"Judge accuracy."** It's circular: it measures agreement with a final order that the judges themselves produced.
- **Self-preference.** Low on average (0.11–0.13), but 0.66 for gpt-5.6-luna and 0.56 for claude-haiku-4.5. Those scores were already dropped from the tally.
- **Control.** It went out in round 1 every time. That only tests the floor: a generic name with no free domains is easy to kill. It says nothing about telling the top names apart.

**Threats to validity:**
- **Not real replications.** The same two names reached all 15 finals. All runs shared one candidate pool and one dossier, so they are resamples of the same models, not replications. Variant C's 3–2 against A/B's 9–1 is too small a sample to mean anything.
- **Shared dossier.** One research pass settled ownability for every judge and included opinions ("strong military connotation"). Any error in it reached every judge.
- **Similar models.** They share training priors that favour Latin and archaic words. No human judged anything.
- **Halving by mean score.** It cuts names that split opinion, and the result was effectively fixed in round 1 (8.06 vs 7.90).
- **Rubric weights.** "Fit" at 30% rewards names that describe the thesis literally. There was no criterion for wryness or trademark risk.
- **"Labs" suffix.** Almost every "Labs" name came back clear and 11 of 16 bare names were blocked or at risk. That tilted ownability scores, which count for 25%.

## 4. One change you'd make first

Check every name both bare and with "Labs", and give judges a dossier of facts only (registry results, domains, named clashes) with no evaluative wording. At present the dossier and the suffix settle most of the result before any judging starts. Until that's fixed, the extra runs, variants and metrics are measuring one research pass, not ten judges.
