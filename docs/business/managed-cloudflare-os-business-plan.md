# Excelcion: demonstrate, solve, license, repeat

Revised strategy — 24 September 2026. This version follows the founders' proposed commercial approach and replaces the earlier managed-service and product-packaging proposals. Suggested trial lengths and working practices below are starting points to test.

**Build things people want to use. Show prospects how those things could work for them. Help them solve their problems, then grow the licence relationship.**

The business combines a platform built on Cloudflare OS with fixed-price consultancy. Consultancy earns money, opens doors and reveals useful things to build. Over time, the platform should attract customers who buy more licences and need less bespoke work. The company remains founder-operated; growing an employee delivery team is not part of the core model.

## 1. The commercial loop

| Step | What we do | What we want to learn or earn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make some shiny things | Build polished applications, integrations and workflows that make the platform's possibilities tangible | Attention, credibility and reusable product assets |
| Get leads | Use existing relationships, introductions, demonstrations and useful public examples | Conversations with people who have a problem and can sponsor adoption |
| Make a tailored presentation | Show how the platform could work with that prospect's processes, systems and teams | A specific reason for them to try it |
| Offer a trial | Provide free or discounted licences for a defined team and period | Real use, feedback, an internal champion and evidence of value |
| Solve their other problems | Offer fixed-price contracts with clear deliverables | Consultancy income, a stronger relationship and, where appropriate, reusable IP |
| Sell more licences | Convert the trial and expand to other users or teams | Recurring platform revenue and wider adoption |
| Find customers needing less consultancy | Use the improved product and accumulated examples to approach better-fitting prospects | More licence revenue per unit of founder effort |
| Repeat | Feed useful work and learning into the next demonstration and sale | A better platform, broader reach and a more scalable business |

These stages can overlap. A prospect may buy consultancy before taking licences, or adopt the platform without any consultancy. A trial does not have to become a project before it becomes a licence sale.

## 2. What “shiny things” should mean

Build a small number of convincing examples that are attractive enough to earn attention and useful enough to start a business conversation. The demonstration should make someone say, “We could use that,” and then ask about their own data or workflow.

Candidate examples from the existing research include a client/project workspace, a reporting application combining operational data, and a knowledge workflow over approved documents. Choose examples the founders can demonstrate well and reuse across several prospects. Do not assume a particular sector or catalogue is settled before those conversations happen.

Each example should have a working demonstration, a short video or screenshots, a plain explanation of the problem it solves, and a clear account of what is available now versus what would need adapting. Use synthetic or authorised demonstration data.

Aim for enough depth to prove the experience, rather than a broad set of unfinished features. The platform and its distinctive applications are what the customer will ultimately license; the demo must lead towards something usable.

## 3. Turn a lead into a tailored proposition

Start with the founders' contacts and people who respond to the demonstrations. Qualify enough to understand their problem, the likely users, relevant systems and the person who could approve a purchase.

A tailored presentation should cover:

1. The problem as the prospect describes it.
2. A concrete example of how their team could work differently.
3. A demonstration or clearly labelled mock-up adapted to their situation.
4. What can be trialled now and what would require additional work.
5. A trial proposal, the likely paid licence structure and any optional consultancy deliverables.

Tailoring the story is part of selling. Building a substantial custom integration is delivery work and should normally be quoted. Reuse presentation components and demonstration assets so each new prospect does not require a fresh unpaid project.

The initial objective is a small, credible next step. A prospect does not need to commit to a company-wide transformation to try one useful application.

## 4. Free or discounted trial licences

Use free or discounted licences deliberately to lower the barrier to adoption. Either is acceptable; choose based on the opportunity rather than imposing a rule that every design partner must pay from day one.

Before starting, agree the users or team, the use case, the support included, a review date and what a paid continuation would cost. Four to eight weeks is a reasonable initial experiment, adjusted for the customer's setup needs. Start measuring the trial when people can actually use it.

Free licences do not automatically include free custom development. If setup requires material bespoke work, quote that separately or explicitly budget it as a sales investment.

Keep the evidence simple: did people activate, did they return, did the application solve the intended problem, and does someone want to keep or expand it? Discuss conversion before the trial ends. Extend a trial for a specific learning or procurement reason, with another decision date.

Ask for feedback, introductions and permission to use successful examples. Reference rights are useful but should be agreed separately; they are not assumed in exchange for a discount.

## 5. Fixed-price consultancy is a real part of the offer

Be willing to solve the customer's other problems, including work outside the platform where the founders have the expertise. It can be profitable work in its own right and a route to a stronger licence relationship. Do not force every project into Cloudflare OS or pretend every deliverable will become a product feature.

A consultancy proposal should state the deliverable, acceptance criteria, dependencies, price, milestones, exclusions and how changes are handled. Where uncertainty makes a fixed build price unreliable, sell a small discovery deliverable first and quote the implementation afterwards.

Distinguish a defect in an agreed deliverable from a new requirement. State what happens after acceptance: any included defect-remediation period, product support where applicable, and the price or handover arrangements for further work. A fixed-price project should not accidentally become an indefinite maintenance commitment.

Where the work produces useful generic components, agree reuse rights in advance and add them to the shared platform. Customer-specific code, confidential information and generic IP should be treated according to the contract. Some projects will contribute mainly cash and relationships; others will substantially improve the product.

Select projects against four practical questions: is the price worthwhile, can we deliver it predictably, does the relationship or learning matter, and what ongoing burden will it create? Consultancy is welcome, but its commitments must fit the founders' capacity.

## 6. Grow the licence relationship

Sell paid licences when people want to keep using the product. Expand when another team or application has a credible use for it. A successful project can reveal that opportunity, but a project sale is not evidence that a licence sale will follow.

Start with a straightforward licence proposal showing what Excelcion adds, which users or deployments are covered, included updates/support, and any separate infrastructure or model costs. Test the existing builder/consumer pricing idea against simpler organisation or user bands. The exact tariff is still a hypothesis; avoid inventing a large tier structure before learning how customers adopt.

Charge for identifiable Excelcion software and capabilities, with clear separation from upstream Cloudflare OS. The licence must offer value independently of access to founder consultancy.

A customer journey might be: a compelling demo leads to a trial for 20 users; a fixed-price integration solves a related problem; the customer buys licences for that team; an internal champion introduces the product to another department. The integration may then make the next customer's trial possible without that project. This is an illustrative journey, not an assumed conversion rate.

## 7. Let the customer mix improve as the product improves

Early customers may need substantial help. That is acceptable if their work funds the business and teaches us what to build. The later opportunity is to find organisations whose needs are already met by the growing platform.

After each engagement, identify what can be reused: an integration, application, installation improvement, document, demonstration or sales argument. Make those assets available in the next release or sale. Look for prospects with the same needs before starting another unrelated bespoke build.

The desired direction is more licence customers, more retained users and less founder effort per adoption. Consultancy need not disappear or even shrink in absolute revenue. Its share of revenue and its importance to each new licence sale should fall as the platform matures.

If each customer still needs a unique implementation, the loop is producing consultancy income but has not yet demonstrated scalable platform adoption. Respond by improving the common product or finding a better-fitting customer group. Do not assume that hiring more delivery people is the solution.

## 8. Operate within the founding team

Plan around the existing founders and no additional employees. Specialist contractors or implementation partners can help with particular work, but expanding an outsourced delivery workforce is not the underlying growth model either.

Use a visible capacity plan covering product work, demonstrations and sales, trials, contracted projects, support and administration. Before accepting a project, identify its delivery slot and the product work it would displace. Limit concurrent commitments and retain time to turn useful client work into shared software.

Do not impose a permanent consultancy percentage before the economics are known. Early consultancy may fund a large share of the business. Review the balance regularly and ensure signed project commitments leave protected time for the platform and the next round of sales.

Product support should be documented and bounded, normally through customer administrators. Custom applications and ongoing operational help need explicit ownership. The business should remain able to support its commitments without a growing service team.

## 9. Measure whether the loop is working

| Stage | Useful measures |
| --- | --- |
| Attention and sales | Qualified leads, tailored presentations, founder preparation time, trial starts |
| Trials | Activation, returning users, time to first useful result, founder assistance required, conversion to paid |
| Consultancy | Contract value, estimated versus actual effort, contribution after delivery costs, ongoing obligations |
| Licences | Paying organisations, paid users, licence ARR, renewal, expansion and actual usage |
| Product reuse | Customers using the same components, fewer custom steps in later deployments |
| Company economics | Cash runway, founder compensation, licence contribution, customer concentration and total committed time |

Track customer cohorts. Do later customers adopt faster, require fewer consulting days and buy more licences? That is stronger evidence than a rising combined revenue total.

In the financial model, separate licence revenue from fixed-price projects and show the founder labour behind both. Include the cost of sales preparation, trial support and free/discounted periods. Licence ARR is annualised contracted licence revenue, not consulting backlog or free seats.

Model the funnel with observed conversion rates as they emerge. Revenue growth must fit the available founder time, and year-end ARR must remain distinct from cash received during the year. A business without employees still needs to fund its founders and product development.

## 10. The next cycle

The immediate plan is to run one complete cycle and learn from it:

1. Choose and polish two or three demonstrations with a clear business use.
2. Put together a short prospect list from existing relationships and incoming interest.
3. Hold discovery conversations and prepare tailored presentations for the strongest leads.
4. Agree small trials with explicit review dates and a paid continuation proposal.
5. Quote suitable problems as fixed-price deliverables, with delivery slots and acceptance criteria.
6. Convert successful trials and identify sensible licence expansion opportunities.
7. Improve the shared product from what we learned, then approach similar prospects with a stronger demonstration.

The first evidence to seek is a paid licence customer who renews or expands, a consultancy project delivered at a worthwhile margin, and a subsequent customer who benefits from the accumulated work with less founder assistance. These may emerge from different accounts; they do not have to occur in a rigid sequence.

## 11. Relationship to the existing research

This plan keeps the original research's consultancy-funded development, enterprise focus, licence ambition and willingness to customise. It makes the proposed sales cycle the organising principle. It supersedes the earlier prescription of managed-service contracts, fixed service-capacity percentages and a predetermined subscription-tier model.

The original research and Atlas remain unchanged. Their next revision should model this funnel and the changing balance between consultancy and licences, rather than growth in a delivery team.
